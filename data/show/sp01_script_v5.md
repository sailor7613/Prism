## 2 · The long 2014 and its end
**TED:** Hello and welcome to the Getty Villa. I'm Ted Turner, and this is a Parallelogram Report special.
I was born in 1992. That's the year Francis Fukuyama published The End of History. The big argument was over. Liberal democracy won.
And he didn't think that was all good news.
**[TAPE — Fukuyama, Charlie Rose 1992, 502.8–530.0]** "…if people say that, I agree with them perfectly… there's a side of man that really wants great causes… struggle and sacrifice… risk and daring."
**TED:** So the end of history wasn't a wall. It was a horizon. And a horizon can move away from you.
Here's how it moved for me. In 2012, Obama told Mitt Romney the 1980s wanted their foreign policy back. We didn't need to worry about Russia. In 2013, I read The End of History. In 2014, Russia took Crimea. And that same year, Fukuyama published a new book. Political Order and Political Decay.
So the culture stalled in 2014, right when history was trying to end itself. It held together for a while. By 2022, with the war in Ukraine, it was grinding.
Then, somewhere between the tariffs and the war with Iran, it broke. The coalitions on both sides came apart. Their contradictions all came out at once. The Epstein files were never fully released. Whatever we all used to agree was underneath everything — that was gone.
Nate's friends started saying the long 2014 was over. I think they're right.

## 4 · The protocol
**TED:** Nate works with computers, and he made a point I keep coming back to.
**[READ — Nate, Contain 68:09]** "I have to mess around with system calls that I can go find in old AT&T manuals from the 1970s. TCP/IP, which the internet runs on — that's barely changed since the 80s. And all these things kind of have ideological assumptions built into them. So social media does as well."
**TED:** The rules underneath us carry the beliefs of the people who wrote them.
In 1992 — the same year as Fukuyama's book — the people writing the internet's rules wrote down their own politics. One of them, David Clark, put it in two lines. We reject kings, presidents and voting. We believe in rough consensus and running code.
Two answers from the same year. Fukuyama said the argument is settled. The engineers said nobody settles it. You build what works, and you keep building.
Engineers have a word for when that stops. Ossification. A system gets so built-in that it can't change anymore. That's the long 2014. The culture ossified. History didn't.

## 5 · Two flops
**TED:** Which brings me to two movies.
Megalopolis, 2024. Francis Ford Coppola put around a hundred and twenty million dollars of his own money into a fable about an architect who can stop time. It flopped.
Digger, this fall. Alejandro Iñárritu and Tom Cruise. An oil man knew a giant ice shelf was going to hit Europe, and he let it happen. It flopped harder.
The same people loved both. The same people hated both. Nate says this about Megalopolis.
**[READ — Nate, Contain 9:34]** "What's interesting is how polarizing the response has been — but it doesn't really follow the typical polarization on political lines."
**TED:** It made people angry without telling them which camp to stand in.
Here's something Prism measures. We call it lumens. Lumens is how much of the actual thing gets through when someone talks. Talk about the thing itself, and the light gets through. Talk about who's on which side, and it doesn't.
And it depends on which way you're facing. Not where you stand — left, right, up, down. Which way you're facing. Turn toward the thing, and you catch light. Turn toward the argument about the argument, and you're in shadow, from any corner.
Digger faces the thing. The oil man stands in front of the European Parliament and says it out loud: you needed the energy, I sold it to you. The light gets in. Then the movie turns away. It tells you it was all a play. Then it walks outside, and the world is a wasteland. So the bill gets paid by the weather.
Megalopolis doesn't confess. It proposes. A city. A new material. A future you can argue with. It walks toward the thing. It's the most hopeful movie I've seen in years.
Nate's host, Barrett, says why you have to catch these when they happen.
**[READ — Barrett, Contain 27:18]** "Eventually somebody comes along later and is able to articulate why it was done, in a post hoc way. And by that time, it's already generally too late. You really have to savor and identify these things in the moment."

## 6 · The nice collapse
**TED:** So here's the question Digger can't answer.
What happens when our institutions admit their contradictions — out loud — and the world doesn't crumble?
No rising water. Maybe the collapse is social. Addiction. People living on the street in Los Angeles.
And what if it isn't even that? What if everything around us stays nice?
Part of why it stays nice: after 2020, the government spent like it had never spent before. There's an idea called Modern Monetary Theory. It says a country that prints its own currency isn't limited by running out of money — it's limited by inflation. After 2020, that went from a fringe idea to roughly how Washington actually behaved. And the inflation that followed was the limit it predicted.
Nate has a word for how it feels to live inside that.
**[READ — Nate, Contain 47:11]** "It feels like this weird inertia that people have been put under. And it seems like the inertia is growing stronger and stronger."
**TED:** Here's how that looks on Prism's map. We read every event from your seat. The American audience. Did it deliver for you?
And from your seat, a lot of it did. The stores are open. The coffee's good. The reading comes up realized.
But the cost of our contradictions didn't go away. It moved. It's on the edge of Europe, in Ukraine. It's in Gaza. So we draw it right beside your reading. Your seat, realized. The cost, somewhere else, frustrated. Same event.
That's the nice collapse. Nothing falls down where you can see it. The wound opens somewhere else.
