## 4 · You deserve better

**Beat 1** (≈ 0:45)
Why are we scrolling?
Honestly. Why are we still looking?
Because we want to pay attention. We want to watch the news. We want to read good journalism. We want to know what's going on.
And we know how rotted out it all is right now.
So here's the thing I want to say first. The political media you're getting doesn't tell you where the power actually is. It's junk.
And you're better than it.

**Beat 2** (≈ 2:30)
I was born in 1992. Same year a guy named Francis Fukuyama published a book called *The End of History*. The argument, roughly: liberal democracy won. The big fights were over.
And that's pretty much how they taught us the world. If you grew up middle class in California in the 90s, the world came with clear terms. One culture. A short list of news everybody watched. And you knew the ladder. The evening news. The LA Times. And if you wanted to think harder, there was The Atlantic, NPR, The New Republic. You could climb.

My dad used to come home with the paper from Starbucks. He'd read it in the morning, then again at night while my mom cooked dinner. And he'd hand me the sections as he finished them. Front page. Business. California.
I was nine on 9/11. I didn't get it at first. Buildings, things happen. By that night I got it.
Then Iraq. And it was all in the paper, right? The link to al-Qaeda was thin. You could read that. My teacher brought in a political cartoon about cutting Social Security to pay for the war, and had us figure out what it meant.
And everybody kind of said, okay. Maybe the case isn't great. But something's going on over there, and we have to do something. We gave it the benefit of the doubt.

That's the pattern. Good faith, extended past the facts. Over and over.
And here's what gets me. None of it is hard to understand. The world they taught us in school is legible. You can read it. And we keep getting told it's too complicated to know.
2008. COVID. If you'd told anyone in 1995 that a Republican president would send almost every American a check, they'd have said that's impossible. That's not how the system works. Then it happened. And then house prices did what they did.
The world keeps doing things the rules said couldn't happen.

And the conversation we get about it? Still capitalism versus communism. Socialism. Fascism. Words thrown around with no regard for what they mean.

Look, the internet did two things at once. It rotted our media. And it also opened everything up. Anybody can publish. People are making weird, funny, sometimes beautiful things every day. Most people can only hold one of those thoughts at a time. You have to hold both.

I'm 34. What's the news going to look like when I'm 60? If it's still people screaming capitalism and communism at each other, it's not going to be better for our kids.
We've been handed all of history. Every record, every book, every photograph. We can see more clearly than anyone ever could. That's exactly why this is so painful to watch.

This isn't about changing the world. It's not a political revolution. It's about giving curious, reasonably literate people a way back into the conversation. One that's worth their time.

## 5 · The modes

**Beat 1 — the moments** (≈ 1:15)
Let's go back to those clips. Three shows. One word.
Ben Shapiro: communism sucks. His proof? California's high-speed rail.
On Tucker, Nathan Apffel: I don't like the word socialist, with the weight it carries. Then he says it anyway.
Jennifer Welch, who hosts the podcast I've Had It, on a clip Gray plays: I wish I didn't have to use the word socialism. And Briahna Joy Gray, on Bad Faith: what they mean by it is, maybe we should have a little healthcare.
Right. Left. Center-left. Everybody's fighting about the word. Nobody's talking about the thing.
So here's the thing. Capitalism, socialism and communism are not three teams. They're three modes. And every economy runs all three. At the same time.
Take the California coast. Capitalism sees lots, hotels, a jetty. Socialism is the town that plans it. Communism is the ocean: in California, the shore below the high tide line belongs to everybody.
Same beach. Same afternoon. The question is never which one. It's where, and how much.

**Beat 2 — the people who already thought about this** (≈ 3:00)
And look. This isn't podcast stuff. Serious people have been here.
1992. Francis Fukuyama, *The End of History*. He looks at South Korea and Taiwan. He admits planning played a bigger role there than here. Then he files the whole miracle under capitalism.
He measures all three modes by one thing: growth. What a society might look like if it wanted something other than more — that never comes up.
Now, this May. Pope Leo the Fourteenth, his first encyclical. *Magnifica Humanitas.* It says the goods of the earth are meant for everybody first. God gave the earth to the whole human race. Private property is real, it says. But never absolute. And then the next principle: groups should provide for themselves, at the smallest level that can handle it. It even puts data and algorithms on the list of things meant for everyone.
That's all three modes. Ranked. From the oldest institution in the West. The Church never bought the either-or.

So let's say them plainly.
**Capitalism** is looking at a pile of rocks and seeing a road. Looking at a row of houses and seeing money. It's the hard reality of surviving and building. You can make anything out of anything.
**Communism** is what we share because none of us chose to be here. Nobody's born with a contract for the mineral rights. Or for language. We don't charge babies to learn to talk. Universal public school is communist. Capitalism needs it — you need people who can read, to take orders and to buy things. Water out of the tap is communist too.
**Socialism** is groups. Kings and queens become executives and philanthropies. Peasants become factory workers and middle managers. A private school is socialist: families pooling money so their kids keep their place. City people, suburb people, people who want families, people who don't. Socialism is how a society shares uneven resources between those groups so the whole thing keeps going. Done badly, it's feudalism.
**Fascism**, while we're here: the state working for capital, and nothing else.

The right and the left already agree on more than they think. Tucker and the Chapo guys are mad about the same things. COVID money. Ukraine. What it costs to live. They just can't talk, because of the words.
So to the right: affordable college, communities setting their own terms — that's socialism. You can have it.
To the left: there's a lot of capitalism in the China you admire. And turning raw material into anything isn't the enemy.

So let's put numbers on it.
