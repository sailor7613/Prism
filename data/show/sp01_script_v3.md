## 2 · History started again
**TED:** Hello and welcome to the Getty Villa. I'm Ted Turner, and this is a Parallelogram Report special. It's the weekend before the midterms. We'll get there.
My friend Nate — Nathan, y2kmindset on Twitter — has a name for the decade we've been living in. He calls it the long 2014.
Here's what he means. Around 2014, the feeds learned that the fastest way to get your attention is to hand you a side. So everything new gets run through two camps. The calendar kept moving. The culture didn't.
I'd put it a little differently.
I was born in 1992. That's the year Francis Fukuyama published The End of History. The idea was simple. The big argument was over. Liberal democracy won.
And in that world, voting meant something. Even when the government wasn't doing the best things, there was a thread you could follow. You vote. They build a consensus. Things get a little better. You vote again. Your one vote tied you to the big story. That's what it felt like, all the way through the Obama years.
In 2014 I was twenty-one. A total normie Democrat. And that's when the middle got tested from every side at once. Bernie Sanders made socialism a normal word again. Gamergate happened. Stories that used to live on the fringe started showing up in serious magazines. Around 2015 I remember reading a left-wing magazine say the Epstein story was real — and also not as big as the right was making it. Real, but not that big. That was the middle holding. Barely.
So Nate says we're stuck in 2014. I think 2014 is when history started again. And nothing that started then has been settled.
Both are true. The machine Nate is describing is what runs while nothing gets settled.

## 4 · 1992
**TED:** So why does this keep happening? Why is every founder a genius or a witch, and never just a person who committed a crime and got a sentence?
In 1992, Fukuyama sat down with Charlie Rose to talk about the book. He used an old Greek word.
**[TAPE — 1992, 283–301]** "…it's the part of the personality that demands recognition from other people. It's the source of self-esteem…"
**TED:** Thymos. The part of you that wants to be recognized. Not fed. Not paid. Seen.
In the book he splits it in two. One side wants to be recognized as greater than other people. He calls that megalothymia. The other side wants to be recognized as equal. Isothymia.
His bet was that a liberal society keeps the first one safe by giving it somewhere to go. Start a company. Make a movie. Be the next big founder.
Then Charlie Rose read him the critics: with no rival left, we're headed for a world of banality.
**[TAPE — 1992, 502.8–530.0]** "…if people say that, I agree with them perfectly… there's a side of man that really wants great causes… struggle and sacrifice… risk and daring."
**TED:** I agree with them perfectly. That's the man who said history was over.
And his bet asked for something else. Good faith. When the system did something that didn't line up — a war sold on reasons that weren't true, a bailout for the banks — you gave it the benefit of the doubt, because it was headed somewhere better. Those were extensions of good faith. They got spent. By 2014, a lot of people had stopped extending them.
So put it together. A young founder who wants to be the next Steve Jobs is megalothymia. A crowd that wants her cut back down to size is isothymia. Once the good faith ran out, there was nothing to hold either one.
They found somewhere to go. The feed. Every day there's someone new to raise up, and someone to burn. It never settles. That's what keeps you there.
